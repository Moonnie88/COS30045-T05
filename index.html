Choosing an energy-efficient TV

Buying a new TV? Its price tag is only part of the cost. Every year it runs, it adds to your electricity bill. These four charts show why running costs matter, and what to look for in the shop.

Wholesale power prices have more than tripled since 1998

Average yearly spot price paid for electricity in Queensland, New South Wales, Victoria and South Australia. Tick a region to add its line. Higher wholesale prices flow through to household bills, so a TV's energy use matters more each year.

At 55", OLED TVs use about 60 kWh more a year than LCD or LED

TVs with more stars generally use less energy

Each dot is a group of models with the same brand, screen technology and size, plotted by its average star rating and labelled energy use.

Most TVs on sale use LED screens

Share of registered TV models by screen technology. This chart counts models, not energy use: a donut shows parts of a whole, and average energy figures do not add up to a whole, so energy by technology is compared in the bar chart instead.

Data: Ex5_TV_energy.csv (COS30045 Exercise 5), models counted by screen technology.

What this means for you

Power is getting more expensive, so a TV's energy use matters for as long as you own it. The recommendations from my TV energy story still hold:

  1. Choose the size you need. Bigger screens use more energy every year.
  2. Compare stars within that size. More stars means lower running costs.
  3. Read the label, not the brand. Check the kWh per year figure, and expect OLED to use more than LED at the same size.
